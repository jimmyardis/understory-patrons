Carolina Observed
South Carolina deserves another natural history.

Within a few hours, blackwater moves beneath cypress; longleaf rises from sand; piedmont rivers break across shoals; salt marsh opens toward the Atlantic; and cove forests climb into the Blue Ridge.

For centuries, naturalists have attempted to describe these places. They drew, collected, mapped, measured, named, and wrote. Their work preserved not only species, but ways of seeing.

Understory begins from the same impulse.

It is a living natural history of South Carolina built from fourteen habitats, species essays, historical plates and maps, commissioned work, authentic field sound, narration, moving image, and a growing collection of Places where ecology, history, and memory meet on the ground.

Mark Catesby
The first great illustrated natural history of Carolina

Nearly three centuries before Understory, Mark Catesby attempted something extraordinary: to describe the plants and animals of Carolina and the neighboring Atlantic world through direct observation, text, and image.

The result was The Natural History of Carolina, Florida and the Bahama Islands — 220 plates accompanied by observations on animals, plants, climate, soil, water, agriculture, and place.

Unable to afford professional engraving for the entire undertaking, Catesby learned to etch much of the work himself.

Mark Catesby · map from The Natural History of Carolina, Florida and the Bahama Islands · Smithsonian Libraries and Archives / Biodiversity Heritage Library · public domain.

And it was made possible by people willing to help finance its creation.

Catesby's work emerged from the British colonial Atlantic world and its systems of power, extraction, and slavery. Understory can draw from the patronage mechanism without romanticizing the society that produced it.
Proposals
For Subscribers to the Natural History
Catesby's proposal soliciting subscribers · late 1729 · Smithsonian Libraries and Archives / Biodiversity Heritage Library · public domain.

Catesby did not wait until the natural history was finished and then hope enough people would buy it.

He circulated a proposal and issued the work in parts of twenty plates.

Subscribers could receive an uncolored part for one guinea or a hand-colored part for two.

The subscriber did not merely purchase a finished book. The subscriber participated in the conditions that allowed the next part to exist.

The Encouragers
A name for the people who made the work possible

In the completed natural history, the people who supported publication were not hidden in an accounting ledger. They were printed into the book.

A List of the Encouragers.

The final list contained 155 names. They included scientists, gardeners, physicians, merchants, collectors, aristocrats, and members of royal households.

Some subscribed for more than one copy. New subscribers continued to join while publication was underway.

The important distinction

They were customers, but they were something more.

Their commitments helped make the next plates possible.

Their names became part of the history of the work because the work existed in part because of them.

John James Audubon
Subscription at monumental scale

A century later, Audubon faced the same fundamental problem at far greater scale.

Birds of America ultimately contained 435 hand-colored plates produced between 1827 and 1838.

The Library of Congress records roughly 300 subscribers who prepaid at some point. A full subscription cost about $1,000 at the time — roughly $35,000 in 2025 consumer purchasing power by the Library's comparison.

About 200 complete folios were produced.

John James Audubon · Wild Turkey · Birds of America, Plate 1 · Rare Book and Special Collections Division, Library of Congress.

The mechanism was not incidental to the art. It was what allowed expensive natural history to be made before a mass market existed for it.

The Continuity
The problem has not changed

A modern natural history still costs more to make well than its retail price can reasonably recover.

Fieldwork · commissioned art · essays · editing · maps · sound · film · narration · accessibility · software · repeated observation

Catesby solved the problem with Encouragers.
Audubon solved it with subscribers.

Understory proposes a modern version of the same compact:

Keep the finished work broadly accessible.

Let a smaller circle of people who value its existence help finance the depth.

This is not sponsorship in the advertising sense.

It is patronage in the older and more durable sense.
